ValidRange – project description

Background

Two hundred years ago, Alexander Von Humboldt set sail for America for a journey that will lay the foundations for scientific exploration. By describing landscapes, species and their habitats, Von Humboldt sketched a revolutionary global picture of the distribution of life [1]. More than two centuries later, and despite an increasingly explored and well-documented natural world, comprehensive estimates of species' elevational ranges remain surprisingly scarce.

Understanding species elevational distribution is a fundamental issue, first and foremost because mountain regions are characterized by exceptional climatic and topographic heterogeneity, this particularity contributing to their status as major biodiversity hotspots, mountains harbouring more than 85% of mammals', amphibians' and birds' species, many of them endemic to their environment([2]. Moreover, mountain environments are among the most vulnerable ecosystems to climate change, experiencing faster warming than the global average [3]. Many of the endemic mountain species are consequently threatened with extinction, but their vulnerability depends both on their range size and on their position along the elevational gradient, emphasizing the importance of accurately documenting their elevational ranges.

Our current limited understanding of species' elevational range across mountains hampers our ability to predict the effects of global change, and consequently to protect mountain ecosystems under future pressure.

Aims

The primary aim of the ValidRange project is to better understand what shapes species' elevational distribution across mountains. To achieve this goal, we first focus on developing a globally consistent assessment of species distributions across mountain regions, information that is currently lacking.

The ValidRange project is a global collaborative effort led by John-Arvid Grytnes between four institutions:

  • the Norwegian University of Life Sciences (Ås, Norway)
  • the Mountains in Motion Research Team (led by Suzette Flantua, Univ. of Bergen, Norway)
  • the Global Mountain Biodiversity Assessment (Bern, Switzerland)
  • the Centre for Mountains in Transitions (Univ. of Bergen, Norway)

The project is building on an initiative launched in 2022 as part of the project "Past, Present, and Future of Alpine Biomes Worldwide" (Mountains in Motion, Univ. of Bergen), and as evidence of the quality and interest of the study, a first paper and corresponding dataset has been published in Nature Communications (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-75210-6), covering plants', mammals', birds', and reptiles' taxonomic groups.

Building on this work, ValidRange is aiming to extend and strengthen this dataset. Specifically, we are increasing the scale of the study by including additional mountain ranges, and including amphibians as an additional taxonomic group. To achieve this goal, we are reaching out to taxonomic experts around the world.

Tasks

General instructions

As an expert, your main task would be to help us validate (and correct if necessary), the lower and upper elevational limits for each species in your taxonomic group and area of expertise.

Please carefully follow these steps in order:

  1. Complete the Google Form provided in the first introductory email you received from us.
  2. You will receive an email once you completed the form. We have unfortunately experienced that many of these emails end up in the spam folder, so please check your spam folder!
  3. Follow the instructions provided in that email. It guides you to the dedicated expert-platform and the access to the relevant checklists.

Expert-platform

Once you created your account on the platform, you have a free and personal access to a dedicated expert platform. You will see on this platform an interactive map of the mountains of the world based on a revised version of the GMBA mountain definition [4]. By clicking on a mountain range and taxonomic group, you will download an Excel file with a list of species from that mountain range. See below (Methods – additional information) for a detailed description of the methods.

Please follow these steps to access the species lists:

  1. Navigate on the map to the mountain range of your choice.
  2. Select the mountain range by clicking on it. Icons for the taxonomic groups will appear.
  3. Click on the taxonomic group of your choice to download the checklist.
  4. Once it is completed, go back to the platform and upload the checklist on the same page.

Specific information on the checklists

Here are the key instructions on how to perform the validation of the checklists:

  1. Validate occurrence: Please confirm whether the species does occur within the mountain range. If the species does occur in that mountain range, leave blank the [presence_corrected] column, if it does not occur in that mountain range, enter "no" in [presence_corrected].
  2. Validate elevation data: The elevational limits have been extracted from relevant literature sources (see Table 1). Review (and correct if necessary) the lower and upper elevational limits for each species within the given mountain range. If needed, enter the corrected lower and upper elevation into the designated columns: [min_corrected] and [max_corrected]. If the current elevation range is correct, indicate this by entering "ok" in the [validated_elevation_data] column.
  3. Assess your level of confidence: Indicate how confident you are in the input you provide on a scale from 1 to 3 in the [confidence_assessment] column – (1) high confidence, (2) medium confidence, (3) low confidence.
  4. Provide comments: Please add any observations, justifications or notes in the [reviewer_comments] column. This could include information on habitat preferences, uneven distribution patterns, or other factors that might influence a species' data accuracy.
  5. Missing species: If you find there are species missing in the spreadsheet, please add them as new rows and add a note "species added" in the [reviewer_comments] column.
Important note: You might find that some mountain ranges are quite wide, and that the species' elevational limits you provide may be non-uniform within this mountain range unit. Some ranges extend over several degrees of latitude and can host very different habitats and climates. If this is the case, please use the [reviewer_comments] column for any complementary information on local patterns. It is indeed essential to bear these limitations in mind, and this work lays the foundations for more in-depth validation in the future.

Structure and column naming of the dataset

Dataset columns - fixed:

  • [sciname/genus/family/order]: taxonomic ranking according to the chosen taxonomic classification (see Table 1).
  • [seasonal]: Only for birds. Seasonality code based on the IUCN mapping standard [5]: (1) resident; (2) breeding season; (3) non-breeding season.
  • [gmba_v2_id]: Unique identification number for the mountain range.
  • [map_name]: Standard name of the mountain range.
  • [min_elevation] / [max_elevation]: The lower and upper elevation limit of the species within the specific mountain range, derived from literature sources (see Table 1).

Expert columns: (see above for specific instructions on how to fill in)

  • [presence_corrected]: Confirmation of the species' presence in the assigned mountain range.
  • [min_corrected] / [max_corrected]: Corrected elevational limits.
  • [validated_elevation_data]: Enter "ok" if the specified elevational range estimate is correct.
  • [confidence_assessment]: confidence level for each species, 1 (high) to 3 (low).
  • [reviewer_comments]: Additional comments on distribution, elevational range, or habitat preferences of the species.

Specific columns for birds:

  • [min_corrected_breeding] / [max_ corrected_breeding]: Correction of the birds' species elevational limits during the breeding season.
  • [min_ corrected_non_breeding] / [max_ corrected_ non_breeding]: Correction of the birds' species elevational limits during the non-breeding season.
Important guidelines for birds' experts:

Correcting elevational limits for birds requires a slightly different approach because many species are migratory. This can make it challenging to define representative elevational limits, particularly in tropical regions where elevational migration is common. You will find four columns for the correction of elevational limits: minimum and maximum elevations for both the breeding and non-breeding season. Please follow these guidelines:

  1. Prioritize the breeding season when correcting elevational limits.
  2. If the breeding and non-breeding season elevational limits are identical, enter the same values for both seasons.
  3. If you are uncertain about the limits for either season, please leave the corresponding fields blank.

Do not hesitate to provide any comments on the [reviewer_comments] column.

Collaboration

By collaborating in this project, you are guaranteed to be included as a co-author of the publications we are planning on writing. For now, two peer-reviewed scientific papers are planned: (1) Global database of species' elevational limits across mountain ranges of the world; (2) Comparison of species' range size variability along elevation in mountain ranges globally.

You would also become part of a global network of regional experts connected to an international, cross-disciplinary platform dedicated to the assessment, conservation, and sustainable management of mountain biodiversity, coordinated by the Global Mountain Biodiversity Assessment.

Methods – additional information

We have compiled elevational ranges of mountain species based on literature and spatial analysis. For the mountain delineations, we used the GMBA Mountain Inventory v2.0 [4], specifically a new version of the "300 selection", a subset of 296 non-overlapping major mountain systems and ranges.

We overlapped the species range maps extracted from literature sources (see Table 1) with the mountain ranges delineations and retained only the species with a distribution range overlapping mountains areas of at least 10 km².

We computed regional elevational limits for each species using three methods: (i) extracted from literature sources (see Table 1); (ii) approximated based on Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) occurrences [6–9] and a 90-meters resolution digital elevation model (Copernicus WorldDEM-90 [10]); (iii) approximated based on their range maps and the same digital elevation model. If no data could be extracted from method (i), we first extracted data using method (ii), and using method (iii) if the two first ones were unsuccessful.
