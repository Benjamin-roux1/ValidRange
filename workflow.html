ValidRange — project description

How the project works, what we're asking of you, and why it matters

Background

Two hundred years ago, Alexander Von Humboldt set sail for America for a journey that would lay the foundations for scientific exploration. By describing landscapes, species and their habitats, Von Humboldt sketched a revolutionary global picture of the distribution of life [1]. More than two centuries later, and despite an increasingly explored and well-documented natural world, comprehensive estimates of species' elevational ranges remain surprisingly scarce.

Understanding species elevational distribution is a fundamental issue, first and foremost because mountain regions are characterized by exceptional climatic and topographic heterogeneity — a particularity contributing to their status as major biodiversity hotspots, with mountains harbouring more than 85% of mammals', amphibians' and birds' species, many of them endemic to their environment [2]. Moreover, mountain environments are among the most vulnerable ecosystems to climate change, experiencing faster warming than the global average [3]. Many endemic mountain species are consequently threatened with extinction, but their vulnerability depends both on their range size and their position along the elevational gradient — emphasizing the importance of accurately documenting their elevational ranges.

Our current limited understanding of species' elevational ranges across mountains hampers our ability to predict the effects of global change, and consequently to protect mountain ecosystems under future pressure.

Aims

The primary aim of the ValidRange project is to better understand what shapes species' elevational distribution across mountains. To achieve this goal, we first focus on developing a globally consistent assessment of species distributions across mountain regions — information that is currently lacking.

The ValidRange project is a global collaborative effort led by John-Arvid Grytnes within the Norwegian University of Life Sciences (Ås, Norway) and the Centre for Mountains in Transitions (Univ. of Bergen, Norway), together with the Mountains in Motion Research Team (Univ. of Bergen, Norway) and the Global Mountain Biodiversity Assessment (Bern, Switzerland). The project was initiated in 2022 as part of "Past, Present, and Future of Alpine Biomes Worldwide" (Mountains in Motion, Univ. of Bergen), and as evidence of the quality and interest of the study, a first paper and corresponding dataset has been published in Nature Communications (doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-75210-6), covering plants', mammals', birds', and reptiles' taxonomic groups.

Building on this work, ValidRange aims to extend and strengthen this dataset. Specifically, we are increasing the scale of the study by including additional mountain ranges, and including amphibians as an additional taxonomic group. To achieve this goal, we are reaching out to taxonomic experts around the world.

Tasks

General instructions

As an expert, your main task is to help us validate (and correct if necessary) the lower and upper elevational limits for each species in your taxonomic group and area of expertise.

Please carefully follow these steps in order:

  1. Complete the Google Form provided in the first introductory email you received from us.
  2. You will receive an email once you've completed the form. We have unfortunately experienced that many of these emails end up in the spam folder — please check your spam folder!
  3. Follow the instructions in that email. It guides you to the dedicated expert platform and access to the relevant checklists.

Expert platform

Once you've created your account on the platform, you have free, personal access to a dedicated expert platform. The first thing you'll see is a map of the mountains of the world, based on a revised version of GMBA maps [4]. By clicking on a mountain range and taxonomic group, you'll download an Excel file with a list of species from that mountain range — see Methods — additional information below for details.

Please follow these steps to access the species lists:

  1. Navigate on the map to the mountain range of your choice.
  2. Select the mountain range by clicking on it. Icons for the taxonomic groups will appear.
  3. Click on the taxonomic group of your choice to download the checklist.
  4. Once it's completed, go back to the platform and upload the checklist on the same page.

Specific information on the checklists

Here are the key instructions for performing the validation:

  1. Validate occurrence: confirm whether the species occurs within the mountain range. If it does occur, leave the presence_corrected column blank. If it does not occur, enter "no" in presence_corrected.
  2. Validate elevation data: the elevational limits were extracted from relevant literature sources (see Table 1). Review — and correct if necessary — the lower and upper elevational limits for each species within the given mountain range. If needed, enter corrected values into min_corrected and max_corrected. If the current range is correct, enter "ok" in validated_elevation_data.
  3. Assess your confidence: on a scale from 1–3 in the confidence_assessment column — (1) high confidence, (2) medium confidence, (3) low confidence.
  4. Provide comments: add any observations, justifications, or notes in reviewer_comments — habitat preferences, uneven distribution patterns, or other factors affecting data accuracy.
  5. Missing species: if species are missing from the spreadsheet, add them as new rows and note "species added" in reviewer_comments.
Important note You might find that some mountain ranges are quite wide, and that the species' elevational limits you provide may be non-uniform within that mountain range unit. Some ranges extend over several degrees of latitude and can host very different habitats and climates. If this is the case, please use the reviewer_comments column for complementary information on local patterns. It's essential to bear these limitations in mind — this work lays the foundation for more in-depth validation in the future.

Structure and column naming of the dataset

Dataset columns — fixed:

  1. sciname / genus / family / order: taxonomic ranking according to the chosen classification (see Table 1).
  2. seasonal: seasonality code (birds and amphibians only), based on the IUCN mapping standard [5]: (1) resident; (2) breeding season; (3) non-breeding season; (4) passage; (5) seasonal occurrence uncertain.
  3. gmba_v2_id: unique identification number for the mountain range.
  4. map_name: standard name of the mountain range.
  5. min_elevation / max_elevation: lower and upper elevation limit of the species within the specific mountain range, derived from literature sources (see Table 1).

Expert columns (see above for how to fill these in):

  1. presence_corrected: confirmation of the species' presence in the assigned mountain range.
  2. min_corrected / max_corrected: corrected elevational limits.
  3. validated_elevation_data: "ok" if the specified elevation range is correct.
  4. confidence_assessment: confidence level for each species, 1 (high) to 3 (low).
  5. reviewer_comments: additional comments on distribution, elevation range, or habitat preferences.

Collaboration

By collaborating on this project, you are guaranteed to be included as a co-author of the publication(s) we're planning. You'll also become part of a global network of regional experts, connected to an international, cross-disciplinary platform dedicated to the assessment, conservation, and sustainable management of mountain biodiversity, coordinated by the Global Mountain Biodiversity Assessment.

Methods — additional information

We compiled elevational ranges of mountain species based on literature and spatial analysis. For mountain delineations, we used the GMBA Mountain Inventory v2.0 [4], specifically the "300 selection" — a subset of 291 non-overlapping major mountain systems and ranges. For species' ranges and elevational limits, we drew on several sources detailed in Table 1.

We selected species with a distribution range overlapping mountain areas of at least 10 km².

GroupElevational rangesDistribution ranges
Mammals Obtained from the machine-readable version of the Handbook of Mammals of the World (HMW) [6] Mammal Diversity Database [7]
Reptiles SquamBase — a database of squamate (Reptilia: Squamata) traits [8] Global Assessment of Reptile Distribution (GARD) [9], [10]
Birds Quintero & Jetz [11] BirdLife International and Handbook of the Birds of the World [12]
Amphibians Guirguis et al. [13] IUCN SSC Amphibian Specialist Group, 2023. State of the World's Amphibians: The Second Global Amphibian Assessment [5]
